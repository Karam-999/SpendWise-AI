# Round 2 — Reflection

## 1. What was the most uncomfortable trade-off you made because of the time pressure?

The most uncomfortable trade-off was sending plain text emails instead of styled HTML emails with formatted pricing diff tables. The notification email is the single touchpoint that brings users back — it *is* the feature from the user's perspective. A well-designed HTML email with a clear visual table showing "Cursor Pro: $20 → $30" with color-coded arrows and a prominent CTA button would have significantly higher click-through than a plain text email with bullet points. I knew this would be the first thing a real product manager would flag. But building and testing HTML emails across Gmail, Outlook, and Apple Mail is a 2-3 hour time sink with no engineering value — it's pure template wrangling. I chose to ship the full end-to-end flow with plain text rather than polish one layer and leave the diff view half-built.

## 2. If we extended the deadline by another 24 hours right now, what's the first thing you'd do?

The first thing I'd do is build the HTML email template with an inline-styled pricing diff table. The email is the funnel entry point for the re-audit feature — if users don't click through, the diff view doesn't matter. I'd use Resend's React email templates (since I'm already on Resend) to create a responsive email showing: a header with the SpendWise logo, a clear "Pricing Changed" headline, a 3-column table (Tool | Old Price | New Price) with red/green highlighting, a savings impact summary line, and a large "View Updated Audit" CTA button. I'd test it against Litmus or Email on Acid to ensure it renders correctly in Gmail, Outlook, and Apple Mail. This is the single highest-leverage improvement because it directly impacts whether users engage with the feature at all.

## 3. Looking back at your Round 1 codebase as a now-experienced user of it: what's one thing your Round 1 self made harder for your Round 2 self?

The hardcoded `PRICING` constant buried inside `auditEngine.ts` as a private `const` was the main friction point. The audit engine was designed as a pure function with no external dependencies — which was the right call for Round 1 — but the pricing data was tightly coupled to the function rather than being an injectable dependency. To make pricing-change detection work, I had to: (1) export the constant, (2) create a `PricingData` type from it, (3) build a parallel `evaluateToolWithPricing()` function that accepts pricing as a parameter, and (4) update 15 internal references from `PRICING.` to `pricing.`. If Round 1 had defined pricing as a separate importable module with its own type, Round 2 would have been a clean plug-in rather than a refactor-then-extend. The lesson: data that might change should always be separated from the logic that consumes it, even if "it's just a constant."
